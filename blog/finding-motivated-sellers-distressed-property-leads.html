FINDING MOTIVATED SELLERS: THE FIVE DISTRESSED-PROPERTY LISTS WORTH WORKING

November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

Knowing your maximum allowable offer is only useful if you have someone to make it to, and in real estate investing the best real estate deals rarely come from the MLS. They come from owners who need to sell for a reason that has nothing to do with the market: a missed mortgage payment, a tired landlord, an inherited house sitting empty, a tax bill that keeps growing.

Those owners leave a trail in public records. This post walks through the five lists investors use most, what kind of motivation each one signals, and how to choose the one you'll work first. If you're still choosing a market, read Is Your Market a Buy, Watch, or Avoid?.

Why Distressed Leads Matter More This Fall

Distress is rising from a low base. ATTOM counted 227,548 U.S. properties with a foreclosure filing in the first half of 2026, up 21% from a year earlier, and 164,566 properties entered the foreclosure process, up 18% (ATTOM Midyear 2026 U.S. Foreclosure Market Report). In August alone, lenders started foreclosure on 25,894 properties, and completed foreclosures were up 42% from August 2025 (ATTOM August 2026 Foreclosure Market Report).

Equity is also thinning. The share of mortgaged homes that are equity-rich (owing no more than half of what the home is worth) fell to 41.1% in Q2 2026, down from 47.4% a year earlier and the lowest in nearly five years, while 3.2% of homes were seriously underwater (ATTOM Q2 2026 Home Equity & Underwater Report).

ATTOM is clear that overall foreclosure activity is still below pre-pandemic norms. So this isn't a flood of distressed property. It's a steady, growing stream, and the investors who already have a list and a follow-up habit will be the ones talking to those owners first.

List 1: Pre-Foreclosures

What it is: Owners who have received a notice of default or a lis pendens (the lawsuit that starts foreclosure in judicial states) but haven't lost the home yet.

Why they're motivated: They're on a deadline, and every month adds late fees and legal costs. Selling before the auction can protect their credit and whatever equity they have left.

What to watch for: Timelines vary enormously by state. Homes foreclosed in Q2 2026 spent an average of 563 days in the process nationally, but Texas averaged 155 days while New York averaged 2,007 (ATTOM Midyear 2026). Know your state's clock before you reach out. Be patient and respectful: this is a hard moment for the owner. Several states also have foreclosure-rescue or equity-purchaser laws that add disclosure and cancellation rules when you buy from someone in default, so check with a local real estate attorney before you make your first offer.

List 2: Absentee Owners

What it is: The owner's mailing address is different from the property address. Usually that's a landlord, sometimes an heir or an owner who moved away.

Why they're motivated: Not always. That's the catch: absentee owners are the biggest list and the weakest signal on its own. The motivated ones are tired landlords, out-of-state owners managing from a distance, and heirs who never wanted a rental.

How to sharpen it: Stack it with another signal. An absentee owner with high equity, a vacant property, or a tax delinquency is a much better lead than an absentee owner alone. Out-of-state mailing addresses and long ownership (10 years or more) are two simple filters that help.

List 3: High-Equity Owners

What it is: Owners who owe little or nothing on the property, often long-time owners or free-and-clear homes.

Why they're motivated: Equity alone isn't motivation, but it's what makes a real estate deal possible. A high-equity owner can accept an offer at your number and still walk away with money, which an underwater owner can't.

Here's why that matters, using last week's example house: an ARV of $300,000, $40,000 in repairs, and a maximum allowable offer of $170,000.

Owner Loan balance Room at your $170,000 offer
Owner A (high equity) $60,000 About $110,000 before the owner's selling costs
Owner B (pre-foreclosure, low equity) $240,000 None. Your offer is $70,000 short of the payoff

Owner B may be far more motivated, but a cash offer at your number can't solve their problem. That lead becomes a short sale conversation with the lender, a referral to a housing counselor, or a pass. Owner A is the one you can actually buy from. That's why the best lists combine distress and equity.

List 4: Vacant Properties

What it is: Homes the post office or utility data flags as unoccupied.

Why they're motivated: An empty house still costs taxes, insurance, utilities, and upkeep, and it's exposed to break-ins and weather damage. Many vacant homes are inherited or left behind by a move.

The numbers: Vacant homes are scarce. ATTOM's latest report found just 1.2% of U.S. residential properties vacant, and of the 269,195 homes in foreclosure, 3%, or 8,040, were "zombies" abandoned before the foreclosure finished (ATTOM Vacant Property and Zombie Foreclosure Report, Oct 29, 2026). Scarcity cuts both ways: a short list, but a strong signal. In some metros the zombie rate runs far higher than the national number, with Baltimore and Youngstown at 13%, so check your own market.

List 5: Tax-Delinquent Properties

What it is: Owners who are behind on property taxes. County treasurers publish or sell these lists, and many post upcoming tax sales online.

Why they're motivated: The county will eventually sell the lien or the property. A multi-year delinquency often points to a bigger problem, such as an estate that's stalled, an owner who has walked away, or someone who simply can't keep up.

What to watch for: One missed bill is noise. Two or more years behind is a signal. Rules and timelines are set county by county, so read your county's tax sale schedule before you build your outreach around it.

How to Pick the List You'll Work First

You don't need all five. You need one list, worked consistently. A simple way to choose:

  1. Match the list to your lane. Wholesalers and flippers usually start with pre-foreclosures, vacant homes, or tax-delinquent properties, where speed and condition create the discount. Buy-and-hold investors often do well with tired absentee landlords who'd rather sell a rented house than manage it.
  2. Stack two signals. Absentee plus high equity, or vacant plus tax-delinquent, cuts the list down to owners who are both motivated and able to sell at your number.
  3. Keep it to a size you can follow up on. A list of 200 owners you contact five or six times beats 5,000 you mail once. Most sellers don't say yes on the first touch.
  4. Run the numbers before you call. Know your comps, ARV, repairs, and maximum allowable offer for the area so you can respond with confidence when an owner is ready to talk.
  5. Follow the rules. Scrub phone numbers against the Do Not Call registry, get consent where the law requires it before texting, and keep every conversation honest and respectful.
