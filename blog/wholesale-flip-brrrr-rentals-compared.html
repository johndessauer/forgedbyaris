WHOLESALE, FLIP, BRRRR, OR RENTALS? HOW TO PICK YOUR STARTING LANE

October 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Every new investor hits the same fork in the road. Wholesale, flip, BRRRR, or buy rentals? The internet will tell you each one is the best, usually depending on who's selling the course. The honest answer is that each strategy asks for a different mix of money, time, and risk, and pays you back in a different way and on a different clock.

We covered the big-picture inputs in How to Choose the Right Real Estate Investing Strategy for You. This post puts the four most common starting lanes side by side, with this fall's market numbers, so you can pick one and commit.

The Four Lanes at a Glance

Wholesale Fix & Flip BRRRR Buy-and-Hold Rental
Capital needed Lowest: marketing and earnest money High: purchase, rehab, and carrying costs (often financed) High up front, part of it recycled at refinance Moderate to high: down payment, closing costs, reserves
Time commitment High and steady: sourcing and negotiating every week High during each project High during rehab, lower once rented Lower once stabilized, especially with a manager
Main risk Deal falls through before you're paid Rehab overruns, holding time, resale price Appraisal and refinance terms Vacancy, repairs, long horizon
How you get paid One fee per deal One profit per project Equity plus monthly cash flow Monthly cash flow plus long-term appreciation and paydown

None of these is the "safe" one. They just put the risk in different places.

Wholesaling: Low Cash, High Hustle

Wholesaling means getting a property under contract and assigning that contract to an end buyer for a fee. It takes the least of your own money, but it's a sales business. You live and die by lead flow, seller conversations, and having buyers ready before you need them. That's why we recommend building your list first, as we walk through in How to Wholesale a Real Estate Deal.

Good fit if: you have more time than money and you're comfortable on the phone.

Watch out for: wholesaling and licensing rules vary by state. Check yours before you market a contract.

Fix & Flip: Real Margins, Shrinking Cushion

Flipping can pay well, but the cushion is thinner than it was. ATTOM's Q2 2026 Home Flipping Report put the typical gross flipping profit at $60,526, a 21.5% gross return, down from 27.6% a year earlier. The median flip took 161 days from purchase to resale (ATTOM).

Here's the catch: that gross figure is just resale price minus purchase price. It doesn't include rehab, financing, holding, or selling costs, which ATTOM notes experienced flippers estimate at 20% to 33% of after-repair value. Your real margin lives or dies on your rehab estimate and how fast you can sell. House Flipping: The Numbers That Actually Matter goes deeper on that math.

Good fit if: you have capital or financing lined up, can manage contractors, and can handle a few months of holding costs.

BRRRR: Recycle Your Capital, If the Numbers Allow

BRRRR stands for Buy, Rehab, Rent, Refinance, Repeat. You buy a property that needs work, fix it, rent it, then refinance based on the new value to pull some or all of your cash back out for the next deal.

Two numbers decide whether that works.

How much a lender will lend. For conventional loans, Fannie Mae caps cash-out refinances on investment properties at 75% of value for one unit and 70% for two to four units (Fannie Mae Eligibility Matrix). Other lenders set their own limits and seasoning rules, so ask before you buy, not after.

What the loan costs. Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed average was 7.40% as of October 15, 2026, up from 6.27% a year earlier (Freddie Mac PMMS). That benchmark is for owner-occupied borrowers with strong credit. Investment property loans are usually priced higher.

A worked example

Illustrative numbers only, not a real deal. Say you buy a single-family house for $120,000, put $40,000 into the rehab, and spend about $8,000 on closing and holding costs. You're in for $168,000. After the rehab it appraises at $220,000 and rents for $1,900 a month.

Assume monthly operating costs of $964: taxes $250, insurance $125, and set-asides for vacancy (8%), maintenance (8%), management (10%), and capital expenses (5%) of rent. Assume a 30-year refinance at 7.90% with $5,000 in refinance costs.

  • Maximum cash-out (75% of $220,000 = $165,000 loan). You get back $160,000 after costs, leaving just $8,000 of your money in the deal. But the principal and interest payment is about $1,199, so the property runs about $263 a month negative.
  • Smaller loan ($120,000). You leave $53,000 in the deal, but the payment drops to about $872 and the property cash-flows about $64 a month.

That's the trade-off at today's rates. Pulling all your cash out can turn a rental into a monthly bill, and leaving cash in slows down the "Repeat." And if the appraisal comes in low, you'll have even less to pull out. Run both versions before you buy.

Good fit if: you have capital to start, can manage a rehab, and want to build a rental portfolio over time.

Buy-and-Hold Rentals: Slow, Steady, Patient

Rentals are the long game: monthly cash flow, loan paydown, and appreciation over years, not months. The biggest day-to-day risk is an empty unit. Nationally, the Census Bureau put the rental vacancy rate at 7.3% in Q2 2026, compared with 7.0% a year earlier (U.S. Census Bureau). Your market can be very different, so check it before you underwrite.

Financing also takes more cash up front. Under that same Fannie Mae matrix, a one-unit investment purchase maxes out at 85% loan-to-value, which means at least 15% down plus closing costs and reserves.

Good fit if: you have a down payment, a steady income, and patience.

How to Choose This Week

  1. Write down your real starting point: cash you can invest today, hours per week, and credit.
  2. Cross off any lane you can't fund or staff. A strategy you can't execute isn't an option yet.
  3. Pick one lane and one market, and give it 90 days before you judge it.
  4. Remember it's a starting lane, not a life sentence. Plenty of investors wholesale to build cash, flip once they have capital and contractors, and hold rentals for long-term wealth.
