# Creative finance lesson outlines

Education only. This is not legal advice, not a loan-assumption approval, and not a promise about what any lender or servicer will do. Members should use their own real estate attorney and, on a specific loan, the servicer’s written answer.

These are the three Empire video paths planned for Lease Options, Subject-To, and Loan Assumption. The calculator that goes with them is `assets/js/creative-finance-calc.js`. It is not wired to a page in this change. The subject-to due-on-sale flag in that calculator stays on. No input, including a trust, turns it off.

GO, CAUTION, and NO-GO use named defaults in `THRESHOLDS` in that file. Residential cash-flow GO matches the long-term rental analyzer: monthly cash flow above $200 and cash-on-cash above 8%, with DSCR at least 1.25. Lease-option GO is a monthly sandwich spread above $200 and a term of at least 12 months. Commercial lease-option GO is a monthly spread above $500. Assumption GO requires the assumed payment to be cheaper than a new loan at the market rate entered on the same balance. Those numbers are the defaults John can change without rewriting the formulas.

## Lease Options

1. **The lease and the option are two contracts.** Goal: separate the right to occupy from the right to buy, and say who holds each one.
2. **Fee, rent, rent credit, strike, term.** Goal: read a term sheet and see which number pays whom.
3. **Straight option versus sandwich.** Goal: know when you never take title, and when the only cash in is the option fee.
4. **Run the spread in the analyzer.** Goal: reject a negative spread, a rent credit larger than the spread, and a strike above the value you are using.
5. **Insurance, disclosures, and state rules.** Goal: leave with a question list for a local attorney. A lease that includes an option to purchase is not the “lease of three years or less” item in the due-on-sale statute (12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d)(4)), because that item excludes a lease that contains an option to purchase. State law can also treat a lease option as an equitable mortgage. This lesson does not conclude the law of any state.
6. **Exercise, assign, or walk away.** Goal: pick the exit before the term starts, including what happens to the option fee.
7. **Commercial lease options.** Goal: price a purchase option against NOI and a cap rate, and name landlord consent.
8. **Master lease options.** Goal: underwrite the spread between master rent and the income you collect, and name consent, SNDA, and estoppel as open items, not boxes you check off inside the calculator.

## Subject-To

The calculator’s due-on-sale flag is on for every subject-to result, residential and commercial. Acknowledging the risk, using a trust, or telling the model the lender will not notice does not clear it. A GO verdict means the cash flow and equity numbers cleared the thresholds. It does not mean the transfer is safe from acceleration.

1. **The deed moves, the loan stays.** Goal: say who holds title and who is still the borrower on the note. The payment does not move the debt into the buyer’s name.
2. **Balance, rate, PITI, arrears, escrow.** Goal: build cash to close from the statement: reinstatement, escrow shortage, cash to the seller, and closing costs. Do not build it from the asking price alone.
3. **Cash to the seller and two kinds of equity.** Goal: separate equity in the property (value minus the loan balance) from equity you are paying for (price minus the loan balance). They are not the same number.
4. **Cash flow and the payment versus a new loan.** Goal: compare the existing PITI with a new amortizing payment at today’s rate on the same balance. The difference is information. It is not permission to transfer.
5. **The due-on-sale clause.** Goal: explain the clause in plain language, the acceleration risk, how lenders actually behave, and what to tell the seller.
   - What it is. A due-on-sale clause is a contract term that lets the lender, at its option, declare the sums secured by the mortgage due and payable if all or any part of the property, or an interest in it, is sold or transferred without the lender’s prior written consent. That is the definition in 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(a)(1). It is the lender’s option. The loan does not automatically accelerate on the day of the deed. The lender has to choose to exercise the clause.
   - Acceleration risk. If the lender exercises it, the full remaining balance can be called due. If that balance cannot be refinanced or paid, the lender can enforce the mortgage, including foreclosure. The seller is still the borrower, so the seller’s credit, and any deficiency the documents allow, sit with the seller. The buyer who only “took over payments” does not replace the borrower by doing so.
   - How lenders actually behave. The Garn-St Germain Act makes a due-on-sale clause generally enforceable notwithstanding contrary state law, subject to the narrow window in subsection (d) and to the contract itself (12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(b)). Servicers do not all read every recorded deed the day it is filed. A transfer is often noticed because insurance changes, mail is returned, a payment is missed, escrow is short, the loan is transferred to a new servicer, someone orders a title search, or a refinance or payoff is requested. Some loans are never called. Some are called quickly. There is no pattern a buyer or a coach can promise. “They rarely call them” is not a term of the loan.
   - What to tell the seller. The loan stays in your name. The buyer’s payments do not remove you from the note. This sale is not a transfer to a spouse or child, a transfer on death, a divorce transfer, or a transfer into your own trust while you remain the beneficiary and occupant. The lender may demand the full payoff. If a payment is late, it is your credit. You should have your own lawyer, and you should not rely on the buyer, a trust, or a course to tell you the lender will leave the loan alone.
6. **Garn-St Germain, 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3.** Goal: state the statute the right way around. The Act is the Garn-St Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982. Section 1701j-3 is titled as a preemption of due-on-sale prohibitions. It is not a subject-to protection statute.
   - Subsection (b) says that, notwithstanding any contrary state constitution, statute, or court decision, a lender may enter into or enforce a contract containing a due-on-sale clause. Except as subsection (d) provides, exercise of the clause is governed by the loan contract. Subsection (b) also says a lender is encouraged to permit an assumption at the existing rate, or at a rate at or below the average of the contract rate and the market rate, and that nothing in the section prohibits such an assumption. Encouraged is not required. The encouragement is not an investor’s right to take a loan over.
   - Subsection (d) is a short list of transfers on which a lender may not exercise a due-on-sale clause. It applies to a real property loan secured by residential real property containing fewer than five dwelling units, including cooperative stock allocated to a dwelling and a residential manufactured home. On those loans, the lender may not exercise the clause upon:
     1. Creation of a lien or other encumbrance subordinate to the lender’s security instrument which does not relate to a transfer of rights of occupancy in the property.
     2. Creation of a purchase-money security interest for household appliances.
     3. A transfer by devise, descent, or operation of law on the death of a joint tenant or tenant by the entirety.
     4. The granting of a leasehold interest of three years or less not containing an option to purchase.
     5. A transfer to a relative resulting from the death of a borrower.
     6. A transfer where the spouse or children of the borrower become an owner of the property.
     7. A transfer resulting from a decree of dissolution of marriage, legal separation agreement, or from an incidental property settlement agreement, by which the spouse of the borrower becomes an owner of the property.
     8. A transfer into an inter vivos trust in which the borrower is and remains a beneficiary and which does not relate to a transfer of rights of occupancy in the property.
     9. Any other transfer or disposition described in regulations prescribed by the Federal Home Loan Bank Board. That board was later abolished and its functions transferred. Subsection (d)(9) is a pointer to regulations, not a blank check. An investor subject-to purchase is not in the statute’s own list. Counsel has to check the current rule. Do not add one.
   - An investor subject-to purchase is not among those exemptions. Title moves to a buyer who is not the borrower’s spouse or child. The borrower does not remain the occupant. The lender may exercise the clause. Subsection (e) also lets the regulator permit a lender to exercise a due-on-sale clause notwithstanding subsection (d) when a related agreement gives the borrower a right to receive future income. Do not treat (d) as a rental loophole.
   - The under-five-unit window matters. The (d) list does not cover a loan secured by property with five or more dwelling units. Commercial and larger multifamily paper is the contract and the loan documents, which is lesson 11.
7. **A trust is for privacy and title holding. It is not a shield from due-on-sale.** Goal: say what a land trust or title-holding trust actually does, and what it does not do.
   - A trust can keep the beneficial owner off the public deed and can spell out who signs and who manages title. That is privacy and title management. It can also be the way a seller and a buyer write down their deal between themselves.
   - The only trust transfer in the statute is subsection (d)(8): a transfer into an inter vivos trust in which the borrower is and remains a beneficiary, and which does not relate to a transfer of rights of occupancy. That is the borrower parking their own property in their own trust and staying there. It is not a buyer’s land trust. It is not a trust in which the seller is a beneficiary on paper while an investor takes occupancy and control.
   - Putting the deed in a trust, or keeping the buyer’s name off the county record, does not put an investor purchase inside (d)(8). It does not stop the lender from exercising a due-on-sale clause. It is not a defense if the lender calls the loan. The calculator does not contain a trust switch, and this lesson does not teach anyone how to make a transfer look like (d)(8) when it is not.
8. **Insurance and servicing.** Goal: name the two operational risks that sit next to due-on-sale. The insurer has to cover the owner who holds the property. The servicer has to be paid, and the seller is still the borrower if a payment is missed. This lesson does not describe a way to leave the seller’s old policy in place so the transfer stays quiet.
9. **When the file becomes a consumer loan.** Goal: recognize that a new loan to an occupant can raise Dodd-Frank and SAFE Act questions, and stop for a lawyer. The lesson identifies the issue. It is not a compliance worksheet.
10. **Hold, refinance, or sell.** Goal: choose an exit that still works if the lender exercises the clause. “They will not notice” is not an exit.
11. **Commercial debt.** Goal: read a transfer restriction, a balloon, recourse or carveouts, and prepay or defeasance. Consent is the deal. The (d) exemptions above are not a 5-unit-and-up safe harbor. If a payoff penalty is larger than the rate savings, do not sell the deal on the savings. The calculator keeps the consent flag and the due-on-sale flag on.

## Loan Assumption

1. **Assumption versus subject-to versus a new loan.** Goal: say who must approve. An assumption asks the lender to accept a new borrower. Subject-to does not. Garn-St Germain encourages lenders to allow assumptions at the contract rate or a blended rate (12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(b)). It does not require them to, except where subsection (d) says they may not call the loan. An investor purchase is not that case. VA, FHA, and USDA assumability comes from those programs’ own rules, not from a general right in Garn-St Germain.
2. **Which loans can be assumed.** Goal: VA, FHA, and USDA can be assumed with servicer credit approval. A conventional loan generally cannot, unless that lender has agreed in writing. The calculator treats a conventional loan as not assumable until that written agreement is marked, and the verdict is then NO-GO.
3. **VA entitlement.** Goal: explain the difference between a veteran who substitutes entitlement and a non-veteran assumer. A non-veteran may be allowed to assume, and the seller’s entitlement can stay used until the loan is paid off or a substituting veteran restores it. The calculator leaves the entitlement flag on unless the assumer is marked as substituting. The flag does not, by itself, force NO-GO. Approval is still required and that flag stays on.
4. **The equity gap.** Goal: cover price minus the assumable balance with cash, an amortizing seller second, or an interest-only seller second, and read the blended payment. A gap that is not covered is NO-GO.
5. **Payment saved versus today’s rate.** Goal: compare the assumed payment with a new amortizing loan at the market rate on the same balance. If the assumed payment is not cheaper, the verdict cannot be GO.
6. **Fees, timeline, and servicer approval.** Goal: put the assumption fee and the wait into the cost of the deal. Entered zero is a real fee. Leaving the fee blank is not the same thing. Approval is not a box the buyer can waive.
7. **Run it in the analyzer.** Goal: produce a verdict from the gap, the savings, and, when it is a rental, the cash flow.
8. **Agency, CMBS, and SBA assumptions.** Goal: budget the fee, the legal cost, and the months, and say whether the seller is released from recourse. CMBS goes through a special servicer. SBA 7(a) and 504 need SBA approval and a buyer who fits the program. A rate reset with no new rate means the calculator will not call the deal a GO and will not claim payment savings. No release of recourse means the seller may still be on the note.
